Interactive Smart Contracts for Secure and
Trustworthy Online Content Consumption Tracking
Shubham Joshi, Dillon Davidson, Zuobin Xiong, and Junggab Son*
Abstract—The rapid evolution of the digital landscape has
made content creation and distribution more accessible than
ever. However, ensuring the integrity and accuracy of con-
tent consumption and download tracking remains a significant
challenge. Centralized tracking systems often lack transparency
and are susceptible to security breaches, raising concerns for
both content creators and service providers. To address these
issues, we propose a blockchain-based solution that leverages
smart contracts for tamper-proof content tracking. Our approach
introduces two secure and interactive smart contracts: an Elliptic
Curve Cryptography-based Proxy Re-Encryption smart contract
(ECC-PRE-SC) and a Counting smart contract (Count-SC).
The ECC-PRE-SC facilitates secure re-encryption, ensuring that
only authorized entities can access the encrypted content key.
Meanwhile, the Count-SC maintains an immutable and verifiable
record of content consumption and downloads. Content Owners
(COs) deploy the ECC-PRE-SC, embedding a list of authorized
service providers’ wallet addresses to enforce access control and
prevent unauthorized modifications to download counts. Each
ECC-PRE-SC instance is linked to a dedicated Count-SC, ensur-
ing precise and independent tracking for specific content. Our
security analysis verifies the system’s robustness against potential
threats, while its successful deployment on the Sepolia Testnet
demonstrates its feasibility. This research enhances trust and
transparency in digital content distribution, providing significant
benefits to content creators, service providers, and end users.
Index Terms —Content Consumption Tracking, Interactive
Smart Contract, Online Content Sharing, Trustworthiness,
Blockchain.

To address the challenge of maintaining integrity and
ensuring a tamper-proof count of content consumption or
downloads, we propose a system that leverages two secure
smart contracts: an Elliptic Curve Cryptography-based Proxy
Re-Encryption Smart Contract (ECC-PRE-SC) and a Counting
Smart Contract (Count-SC). The ECC-PRE-SC manages the
re-encryption process as part of granting access to content

the ECC-PRE-SC is executed successfully.

Count-SC within the ECC-PRE-SC contract. This triggers the

• To the best of our knowledge, no prior DRM work

II. R ELATED WORK
This section explores recent research trends in secure
content sharing within cloud environments. We specifically
examine studies focused on encryption schemes, cloud content
sharing mechanisms, and the application of smart contracts
within DRM systems.
A. Blockchain and Counting in Other Domains
Blockchain-based counting mechanisms span several do-
mains, including supply chains, advertising, and voting. In
supply chains, platforms such as IBM Food Trust ensure
immutable traceability of goods, capturing both provenance
and quantitative data [12]. Westerkamp et al. introduced token
recipes to formalize manufacturing transformations, preserving
quantity conservation [13], while bipartite-matching frame-
works provides one-to-one tracking of product flow [14]. In
advertising, B²DAM immutably logs campaign exposures [15],
whereas CountChain provide transparent exposure reporting
by recording campaign events and validating off-chain impres-
sions [16], though these approaches ultimately depend on or-
acle consensus or external reporting rather than cryptographic
enforcement.
In voting and provenance management, blockchain func-
tions as a tamper-resistant ledger that enhances transparency
and auditability. Hyperledger-based voting systems with
Shamir secret sharing and IoT-integrated e-polling frameworks
focus on securing ballots and ensuring correct tallying [17],
[18]. Immutable logging solutions, such as Ozdayi et al.’s
cross-site access auditing [19], leverage blockchain to provide
verifiable audit trails, where counting arises through aggre-
gation of logs rather than cryptographic coupling of events
to counters. Collectively, these efforts highlight blockchain’s
capacity to ensure trust and accountability in contexts where
tallying is central, with varying reliance on on-chain mecha-
nisms versus off-chain validation.
These prior efforts illustrate blockchain’s suitability for
transparent, tamper-proof record-keeping across domains such
as supply chain, advertising, voting, and logging. However,
they differ fundamentally from our approach: in those sys-
tems, counters increment based on reported events or oracle
validation, whereas in our design, a counter update is only
possible as a by-product of a zero-knowledge-verified proxy
re-encryption that yields a usable content key. Thus, con-
tent consumption counts are cryptographically guaranteed to
correspond to genuine access events, not merely asserted or
reported.
B. Cloud Content Sharing
Several studies address the challenges of secure cloud
content sharing. Zhang et al. introduced CREAMS, a scheme
prioritizing copyright protection and data privacy within media
sharing contexts [20]. Their design combines additive homo-
morphic proxy re-encryption with asymmetric fingerprinting
techniques to address the problem of data privacy leakage,
infringement on the data owner’s copyright.

3
Feng et al. proposed a proxy re-encryption scheme com-
bining attribute-based and identity-based encryption, inte-
grating blockchain to prevent data tampering and eliminate
reliance on trusted third parties [21]. Liu et al. improved
efficiency by removing bilinear pairs and addressing certificate
management and key escrow issues through elliptic curve
groups, certificate-based encryption, and identity-based proxy
re-encryption [22]. Both schemes enhance security and effi-
ciency while preventing unauthorized data exposure.
Wang et al. proposed a blockchain-based secure data-
sharing scheme for the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT),
enabling anonymous cross-domain access for authorized de-
vices [23]. By combining broadcast encryption and proxy re-
encryption, their scheme ensures secure, flexible data sharing
while preserving privacy, though re-encryption relies on a
cloud-based trusted third party.
Prior cloud content-sharing schemes emphasize confiden-
tiality, policy-driven access, and privacy—via homomorphic or
attribute/identity-based proxy re-encryption, watermarking/fin-
gerprinting, and occasionally blockchain for provenance, but
they do not cryptographically ensure that usage reports equal
genuine accesses. In particular, they (i) leave consumption
counting to mutable platform software, (ii) lack a mecha-
nism that forces a counter increment to be an unavoidable
consequence of delivering a real, decryptable key, and (iii)
sometimes retain a trusted re-encryption proxy, re-introducing
unilateral control.
C. DRM based on Smart Contracts
The potential of blockchain and smart contracts in DRM
systems has been demonstrated in several studies. Zhang
et al. addressed limitations of traditional centralized DRM
by employing smart contracts for reliable copyright transac-
tions [24]. Garba et al. proposed a distributed media trans-
action framework combining digital watermarking with a
scalable blockchain model to secure authorized access, protect
offline content, and enable copyright reclamation in case of
leakage [25].
Gao et al. introduced a blockchain-based DRM scheme
using Multi-Authority Ciphertext-Policy Attribute-Based En-
cryption (MA-CPABE) and proxy re-encryption to enhance
confidentiality and support efficient copyright transfers [26].
Likewise, Kemmoe et al. leveraged smart contracts for secure,
asynchronous group key exchange, improving flexibility in
rights management [27].
While these works highlight blockchain’s potential for DRM
and secure key exchange, they do not enforce verifiable
consumption counts and often rely on honest reporting. Our
approach addresses this gap by coupling proxy re-encryption
with a zero-knowledge-verified counter: the Count-SC in-
crements only as a by-product of successful re-encryption
in the ECC-PRE-SC, ensuring tamper-proof and auditable
tracking. This eliminates unilateral trust in service providers
and introduces immutable, cryptographically enforced usage
verification, overcoming critical shortcomings in prior DRM
frameworks.
D. Encryption Schemes for Secure Data Sharing
Duan et al. proposed an attribute-based proxy re-encryption
scheme that supports re-encryption, access delegation, and
blockchain-based logging through smart contracts [28], but
it does not secure usage counts. Hussain et al. introduced
a lightweight certificate-based signcryption with proxy re-
encryption (CBSRE) using hyperelliptic curves for IoT-
enabled smart grids [29]. Similarly, Agyekum et al. combined
identity-based encryption with PRE to enhance IoT access
control [30], while Keshta et al. integrated blockchain with
PRE via the SM2 algorithm to improve data privacy [31].
Shabisha et al. explored ECC-based signcryption and PRE
techniques to strengthen cloud data storage against curious
providers [32].
These schemes collectively provide robust confidentiality,
flexible access delegation, and efficient proxy re-encryption.
However, none cryptographically bind re-encryption opera-
tions to an immutable counter or prevent service providers
from misreporting usage. As a result, they leave consumption
accountability dependent on platform honesty or off-chain
mechanisms.
In contrast, our approach integrates ECC-based proxy re-
encryption with a blockchain-resident counting contract. Each
valid content access or data retrieval unavoidably and ver-
ifiably increments the counter. By embedding authorized
provider addresses within the ECC-PRE-SC and employing
zero-knowledge proofs to gate re-encryption, our scheme
eliminates trusted intermediaries and ensures tamper-resistant,
auditable consumption tracking, addressing critical account-
ability and transparency gaps in prior work.

A. Blockchain and Smart Contracts
Blockchain is a sequentially linked data structure oper-
ating in a peer-to-peer network with a reliable consensus
mechanism, ensuring security, traceability, decentralization,
and trustlessness. As a distributed ledger, it records verified
transactions with timestamps, making data immutable and
publicly verifiable. These features enable applications that
function without trusted intermediaries [33] [34].
Smart contracts are decentralized programs that automate
transactions between parties without a central authority [35].
Various platforms support smart contract development, includ-
ing Bitcoin [36], Ethereum [37], Hyperledger Fabric [38], and
Stellar [39], with Ethereum being the most widely adopted
due to its stability and security [40].

IV. P ROBLEM DEFINITIONS
A. System Model
Fig. 1 illustrates the proposed system model. The model
considers a scenario with a CO and a single blockchain net-
work, reflecting a realistic setting where CO want to distribute
their content through various SPs. It also considers multiple
users who can interact with SPs to access content. The model
consists of five entities: Trusted Third Party ( TTP), Con-
tent Owners( COs), Service Providers ( SP1, SP2, . . . , SPn),
Blockchain Network ( BN), and Users ( U1, U2, . . . , Un).
1) Trusted Third Part (TTP): The TTP is responsible for
setting up the public parameters and generating key pairs for
the COs, SPs, and users. It securely distributes the public keys
and private keys to the respective parties.
2) Content Owner (COs): The CO produces content like
music, videos, and ebooks, encrypts it with a unique content
key, and distributes it via SPs. The CO generates the content
key which is used to encrypt the content. Furthermore, the
CO deploys two smart contracts in the blockchain with the
encrypted content key and authorized SPs wallet addresses.
For every content, a unique content key is used for encryption,
and consequently, a distinct smart contract is deployed for
each.
3) Service Providers (SPs): SPs receive encrypted content
from COs and distribute it via their platforms. They generate
re-encryption keys and interact with ECC-PRE-SC to obtain
re-encrypted content keys, which are then forwarded to users
along with the encrypted content.
4) Blockchain Network (BN): BN consists of two smart
contracts: ECC-PRE-SC, which verifies SPs using zero-
knowledge proofs and re-encrypts content keys, and Count-SC,
which tracks content consumption. As a decentralized ledger,
it ensures tamper-proof data for COs and SPs.
5) Users: Users register with SPs to access encrypted
content. After authentication, a user Ui receives the encrypted
content and a re-encrypted key, which can only be decrypted
with their private key. This allows them to retrieve the content
key and decrypt the content securely.
B. Threat Model
The following models represent the list of attacks that may
target to our system model.
• Threat Model 1: An adversary may analyze multiple re-
encrypted content keys on the blockchain to deduce the
content key. By locating transaction details via transaction
hash, block hash, or smart contract address, they may
attempt to infer the key and decrypt the content.
• Threat Model 2: A CO or SP may act as an adversary and
attempt to maliciously manipulate the count recorded on
the blockchain, seeking to change the count for personal
gain. An unethical attempt to gain control over the Count-
SC may be made in order to achieve this goal.
• Threat Model 3: A User with legitimate access to one
content may attempt to leverage this access to gain
unauthorized access to a different content. A User with
a legitimate content key to one of the contents may

key.
• Threat Model 4: The SP may act as an honest but
curious entity seeking to retrieve information through
the transactions that go through it. The SP intermediates
re-encryption key creation and forwards encrypted and
re-encrypted content to Users. The SP may attempt to
obtain information regarding the content key through this
process.
• Threat Model 5: An adversarial entity may attempt to
impersonate an honest SP to request the re-encrypted con-

the ECC-PRE-SC to disrupt its functionality.
C. Design Goals
Considering the described system model and threat model,
the following design goals has been established.
• The system aims to record and display the tamper-proof
count of the number of content consumed or downloaded.
• The system aims to implement privacy-preserving tech-
niques to anonymize or hide information that can other-
wise be seen on the blockchain.
• The system aims to follow key independence, ensuring
that there are different content keys for different contents,
and they must be random during key establishment.

A. Simulation Specifics
The SECP256k1 elliptic curve was used for all elliptic curve
point operations. Our program was written and compiled using
Solidity 0.7.26 and the elliptic curve arithmetic operations are
implemented with the elliptic-curve-solidity and FastEcMul
library. We used the Sepolia Blockchain Network and the
Alchemy API to connect to the blockchain instance to simulate
our scheme. The deployment and interaction were performed
using Python on a computer with an Intel i7-1355U running
Windows 11.
B. Performance Analysis
For the following analysis, we let l = 128 bits, where l is the
length of the key which is used to encrypt the content. In our
case, the plaintext data that will be encrypted is a content key,
so we let n = 128, 192, and 256 bits, respectively, representing
various length keys based on key size recommendations for
symmetric key lengths for the AES encryption algorithm [48].
As shown in Fig. 4, the size of the message is largely irrelevant
in terms of overall gas consumption. The principal driver of
gas consumption is the execution of the four elliptic curve
scalar multiplication operations (with a 256-bit scalar) and one
elliptic curve point addition operation that are all needed to
verify and compute the re-encrypted ciphertexts in the Re-
Encrypt function. Overall, the ECC-PRE-SC has a consistent
gas cost with little variance regardless of the actual message

length, so our scheme has great flexibility in the size of the
desired re-encrypted message. The counting contract Count-

keeping a count. Retrieving the current count for a specific

as per our verification, the transaction cost for the increment

In future work, this cost could be estimated using dedicated

While direct, standardized comparisons across privacy-
preserving Ethereum contracts are not available, indicative
gas-cost ranges from prior ZKP-based systems provide useful
context. Our ECC-PRE–SC implementation on Sepolia incurs
1.66 M gas per transaction, which includes both elliptic-curve
proxy re-encryption and embedded zero-knowledge verifica-
tion. By contrast, previously reported values for contracts
focused solely on ZKP primitives range from about 1 M
gas for a simple zk-SNARK verification (e.g., zkay [49]) to
7.19 M gas for Bulletproof-based transfers (e.g., Zether [50]).
These figures suggest that, even after incorporating additional
ECC operations for proxy re-encryption, our design operates
within a competitive cost envelope—substantially lower than
Bulletproof-based schemes and only modestly above a min-
imal zk-SNARK verification, while providing stronger func-
tionality. The tight integration of ZKP verification with proxy
re-encryption, rather than invoking separate heavy proof-
verification contracts, enables our approach to preserve privacy
and enforce tamper-proof counting with practical on-chain
efficiency, supporting feasible throughput under Ethereum’s
15–30 M gas block constraints without sacrificing verifiable
content access tracking.

C. Performance Improvement
During implementation, we observed that the initial version
of the re-encryption scheme consumed significantly more gas
due to multiple ECC-based operations, including multiplica-
tions and additions. The multiplication operation, being the
most expensive, prompted us to identify a different library
that provides speed-ups for ECC multiplication using window
Non-Adjacent Form (wNAF) scalar representation and scalar
decomposition through endomorphism [51]. Specifically, the
gas consumption decreased from 455 × 104 to 166 × 104 for a
128-bit content key length, from 461×104 to 166×104 for 192
bits, and from 453×104 to 163×104 for 256 bits. This results
in an average gas consumption reduction of approximately
63.84%. The comparison of the gas consumption for our first
version of the system and the second improved version has
been shown in the Fig. 4.
D. Lessons Learned
During the optimization and validation of our smart con-
tracts, several important lessons were identified that may ben-
efit future implementations. First, repeated access to storage
variables, such as array lengths, should be avoided by caching
values locally to reduce gas consumption. Second, employing
optimized elliptic curve libraries that utilize techniques such as
window Non-Adjacent Form (wNAF) scalar representation and
scalar decomposition through endomorphism can significantly
lower the cost of elliptic curve operations. Third, declaring
immutable state variables when their values are set only once
reduces storage overhead and deployment costs. Finally, vali-
dation checks, such as preventing zero-address assignments,
are essential for ensuring contract robustness. Collectively,
these practices improved both the efficiency and security of our
contracts and can serve as practical guidance for developers
aiming to write gas-efficient Solidity code.
